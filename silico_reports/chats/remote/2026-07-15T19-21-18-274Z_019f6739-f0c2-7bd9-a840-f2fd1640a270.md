Found it — Covenant-72B is the Templar/Bittensor decentralized run: 72B dense, 1.1T tokens of commodity-quality data, self-benchmarked around LLaMA-2-70B / LLM360-K2 class. That matters for your question, because it sets the bar low: a modern 72B on 10T+ curated tokens already clears Covenant by a wide margin. The interesting comparison isn't "beat Covenant," it's "beat a same-cost MoE."

**On the economics of mechinterp at the 1T tier:** the underappreciated fact is that interp cost tracks the residual stream width and active params, not total params. Inkling's stream is ~7–8k wide — the same order as a 70B dense — so SAEs, probes, and geometry on harvested activations cost the same as they would on a 70B. The 1T tax lands in exactly two places:

- **Serving for rollouts** (2–4 nodes, but one-time per eval batch, and the layerwise-replay trick makes harvest itself ~1 node), and
- **Causal validation** — every ablation or patching forward needs the full model resident. Describing a 1T model is cheap; *proving* causality on it costs 4 nodes per intervention batch.

So the economics are program-shaped: the weights download and harvest amortize across every question you ask afterward, which makes intensive mechinterp on 1T models fine if you run many analyses per harvest, and terrible for one-offs. The real strategic annoyance is that Inkling and Kimi ship with no smaller siblings — no family ladder to validate mechanisms cheaply and port up, the way you can with Qwen. One genuine 1T-tier bonus: MoE routers hand you free interpretable structure (discrete routing decisions, expert co-activation) that dense models don't have — though at 256 experts × 66 layers, expert-level circuit work multiplies the component count brutally.

**On dense-72B + routed role-adapters as a MoE alternative:** the honest capability read is that token-level MoE routing wins pretraining loss per FLOP because it adds *storage capacity* without active-FLOP cost. Role-level adapter routing doesn't — adapters modulate behavior, they don't hold trillion-token knowledge. On SimpleQA-style evals, the hybrid loses to a 1T MoE, and no routing cleverness fixes that.

But the hybrid wins on axes your program actually cares about:

- **Auditability**: an adapter is a small, diffable, signable object. That plugs straight into the RSITopology edit-gating machinery — certifying an adapter swap is tractable in a way certifying an opaque full-model update isn't.
- **Control as architecture**: your storyworld role-shift question ("does a hidden trait survive the archivist→auditor transition?") becomes architectural rather than prompted. Does the trait persist across an *adapter swap*? That's a crisper, causally cleaner experiment than prompt-role switching, and probes can watch it directly.
- **Interp economics**: everything fits on one node, and the adapter delta *is* the model diff — model-diffing for free.

The "tiny models adapting learning" piece is arguably the strongest part of your sketch — small non-LLM routers/monitors deciding which adapter regime is live (you already have a TRM router stub in the symbolic env). Where the literature stands, adapter-composition approaches match specialized fine-tunes at a fraction of the cost but don't recover MoE-scale generality — so I'd frame the hybrid not as "better than a 1T MoE" but as "a controllable, certifiable specialist that beats Covenant-class dense models easily and trades away encyclopedic breadth."

There's a cheap weekend-adjacent experiment hiding in this, for whenever GPUs free up: train role adapters on a mid-size dense model for the storyworld roles, then measure trait persistence across adapter swaps vs prompt swaps with probes. Single node, and it would tell you whether role-routing actually gives the containment properties the architecture argument promises.
